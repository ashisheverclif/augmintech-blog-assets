Space planning is the process of deciding where every activity, room and piece of furniture goes so a space works well before it looks good. It moves from the brief to an adjacency study, zoning into public, private and service areas, circulation, and finally a dimensioned furniture layout checked against minimum sizes and clearances. In India, NBC 2016 sets minimums such as 9.5 m² for a single habitable room and 5.0 m² for a kitchen. This guide walks through each step, gives the key numbers, and shows worked layouts for a 2BHK, a bedroom, a kitchen and a small office.

TL;DR

  • Plan before you decorate: zoning, circulation and furniture fit decide whether a space works.
  • Follow a process: brief, measure, analyse, adjacency, zoning, circulation, layout, test.
  • Separate public, private and service zones so guests, family and services do not cross.
  • Know the minimums: under NBC 2016, a single habitable room is at least 9.5 m² and 2.4 m wide, a kitchen 5.0 m² and 1.8 m wide, a bath-plus-WC 2.8 m².
  • Respect clearances: NKBA recommends 914 mm walkways and 1,067 mm work aisles in kitchens.
  • Draw to scale and test in 3D before the client sees it.

What Space Planning Is, and Why It Comes First

Space planning sits between the brief and the design details. It answers practical questions: where the sofa goes, whether two people can pass in the kitchen, whether the bed blocks the wardrobe, whether guests walk past the bedrooms. A beautiful material palette cannot rescue a plan where the dining table blocks the balcony door. Good planning also saves money, because fewer changes are needed once carpentry and services begin.

Planning works at three scales: the whole home or floor (zoning), each room (layout), and each piece of furniture (clearances). You move between them constantly.

The Space Planning Process in 8 Steps

  1. Write the brief and the programmeList users, activities, rooms, storage needs, furniture to keep, budget and must-haves. A programme turns the brief into a list of spaces with approximate areas.
  2. Measure the site accuratelyWall-to-wall dimensions, ceiling heights, beams, columns, windows, doors, plumbing and electrical points. A wrong measurement here ruins every later step.
  3. Analyse the shellMark daylight and views, noise, ventilation, structural walls you cannot move, and fixed services such as shafts and drains.
  4. Map adjacenciesDecide which spaces must be next to each other, which should be near, and which must be apart. A bubble diagram or adjacency matrix captures this.
  5. Zone the planGroup spaces into public, private and service zones, placing each where light, access and services suit it.
  6. Plan circulationDraw the main paths from the entry. Keep them short, direct and clear of furniture, and avoid routes through private rooms.
  7. Lay out furniture to scalePlace real furniture sizes, check clearances and door swings, and test two or three options.
  8. Test and refineCheck against minimum sizes and codes, model it quickly in 3D, walk through it with the client, and revise.

Zoning: Public, Private and Service

Public zoneFoyer, living, dining. Near the entry, with the best light and views, where guests are received.
Private zoneBedrooms, study, family lounge. Away from the entry and noise, reached without passing through public rooms.
Service zoneKitchen, utility, toilets, store. Grouped around plumbing shafts and with direct access for deliveries and waste.

Minimum is not comfortable: these are legal floors, not targets. A 9.5 m² bedroom fits a double bed and a wardrobe only with careful planning; most designers aim higher where the plan allows.

Clearances and Circulation

Clearances are the space people need to move and use furniture. The most widely used figures for kitchens and dining come from the NKBA's planning guidelines:

SituationRecommended clearance
Walkway914 mm (36 in) minimum
Kitchen work aisle, one cook1,067 mm (42 in)
Kitchen work aisle, more than one cook1,219 mm (48 in)
Behind a seated diner, no traffic813 mm (32 in)
Behind a seated diner, people edging past914 mm (36 in)
Behind a seated diner, people walking past1,118 mm (44 in)
Landing beside the sink610 mm one side, 457 mm the other

Hallway within a home (US IRC reference)915 mm (36 in)

NKBA Kitchen Planning Guidelines; hallway figure from the US International Residential Code, as summarised by Learn Architecture. Use them as good practice alongside Indian codes.

Beyond the numbers, three circulation rules matter in every plan: keep the main path free of furniture, never route circulation diagonally across a room's usable centre, and check every door swing against furniture, other doors and switches.

Confirm daylight, ventilation, plumbing shafts, structure and applicable local requirements. The schematic does not establish accessibility, minimum clearances or code compliance.

Example 2: The bedroom

  • Place the bed first, with the headboard on a suitable wall. Check window operation, privacy and access rather than treating one orientation as a universal rule.
  • Keep walking space on both sides of a double bed and at the foot, so two people can get in and out without climbing over.
  • Check the wardrobe: leave room for hinged shutters to open fully, or use sliding shutters in tight rooms.
  • Plan the switches: bedside switches for lights and fan at both sides of the bed.
  • Keep the window clear for light and ventilation, and avoid the door hitting furniture.

Example 3: The kitchen

Choose the layout from the room width. A single straight run fits the 1.8 m minimum width; an L-shape suits a near-square room; a parallel or U-shaped kitchen needs about 2.3 m or more so the 1,067 mm work aisle survives. Keep sink, hob and fridge within an efficient triangle (NKBA suggests each leg between 1.2 and 2.7 m, totalling no more than 7.9 m), with landing space beside the sink and hob as in the table above.

Example 4: A small office

Office planning works from people and desks. Studio Matrx's 2026 benchmarks, which it describes as illustrative, put typical gross area at about 10–12 m² per person including meeting rooms, circulation and support space, and open-plan desks at about 6–8 m² of usable area each including local circulation. Bench positions range from a compact 1.4 m to a generous 1.8 m width, and with hybrid working, seven or eight desks per ten people is a common sharing ratio.

8 Common Space Planning Mistakes

  1. Designing before measuring. Planning on a builder's brochure plan instead of site dimensions.
  2. Using furniture that is not to scale, or downloaded blocks with unrealistic sizes.
  3. Forgetting door swings, including wardrobe, appliance and balcony doors.
  4. Circulation through the middle of rooms, leaving no usable space.
  5. Ignoring structure and services: beams, columns, shafts and drain points fix where some things must go.
  6. Too little storage, the most common complaint in Indian homes after move-in.
  7. Blocking light and ventilation with tall furniture in front of windows.
  8. Treating minimums as targets, producing rooms that pass code but feel cramped.

Space Planning for Gulf Homes and Offices

Gulf projects bring their own planning patterns: open kitchens are common in apartments, while larger villas often add a closed "dirty kitchen" for heavy cooking, majlis rooms for receiving guests separately from the family zone, and maid's rooms with their own access. Offices and hotels are often planned in BIM: since 1 January 2024, Dubai has required BIM model submissions in IFC format for building permits on larger buildings, as summarised on Autodesk's Dubai BIM mandate page.

Worked Check: Does the Furniture Actually Fit?

Take a fictional rectangular room with an internal width of 4,000 mm. A sofa projects 900 mm from one wall and a storage unit projects 450 mm from the opposite wall. The width remaining between their faces is 4,000 − 900 − 450 = 2,650 mm. A coffee table, occupied seating and circulation must all fit within that remainder. This arithmetic is a first check, not proof that the layout is accessible or comfortable.

Next, test movement: draw the entrance swing, open cabinet doors, pull out a chair and trace the route to the window and storage. Use actual product dimensions. A layout that works with closed cupboards and empty chairs can fail during use.

Compare Options Against the Same Brief

Keep the shell, openings and user needs identical in both options. Compare circulation, furniture access, daylight, privacy, service relocation and storage. Write one advantage and one compromise for each. Do not decide from the perspective image alone: a wide camera lens can make a tight room appear generous.

What to Do When the Plan Fails

First remove unnecessary furniture or choose a different footprint. Then test a different orientation, storage arrangement or door strategy where permissible. Do not quietly reduce the clearance assumption to preserve a preferred design. Record unresolved constraints and verify the appropriate requirements before finalising the layout.

The BIS overview describes NBC as a model code for adoption. The CPWD guide to NBC 2016 summarises selected room provisions. Read the applicable clauses and local adopted rules; minimum area alone does not establish a usable or compliant room.

Space Planning Checklist

Brief and programme agreed and written down.
Site measured and drawn, with structure and services marked.
Adjacencies and zones resolved before furniture.
Circulation short, clear and not through private rooms.
Rooms checked against NBC minimums and local by-laws.
Clearances and door swings checked at every piece of furniture.
Storage counted for every room.
Tested in 3D and walked through with the client.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is space planning in interior design?
It is the process of organising a space for its users: deciding which activities go where, how people move between them, and how furniture fits, before choosing finishes and decor.
What are the steps of space planning?
Brief and programme, site measurement, analysis of the shell, adjacency study, zoning, circulation, furniture layout to scale, and testing against codes, clearances and the client's needs.
What is the minimum size of a bedroom in India?
NBC 2016 sets 9.5 m² with a minimum width of 2.4 m for a single habitable room. Local by-laws can differ, so check them for each project.
What is the minimum kitchen size in India?
A separate kitchen should be at least 5.0 m² with a minimum width of 1.8 m under NBC 2016; a kitchen-cum-dining room at least 7.5 m² and 2.1 m wide.
How much space is needed between furniture?
NKBA recommends at least 914 mm for walkways, 1,067 mm for a one-cook kitchen aisle, and 914–1,118 mm behind dining chairs where people pass.
How much office space is needed per person?
Studio Matrx's illustrative 2026 benchmarks suggest about 10–12 m² gross per person including support space, and 6–8 m² of usable area per open-plan desk.
Which software is best for space planning?
Start on paper for zoning, use AutoCAD for accurate layouts and SketchUp to test in 3D. Revit adds rooms with automatic areas and schedules, useful on larger projects.
