NPS sets the OD

Every pipe of the same nominal size has the same outside diameter, whatever its schedule.

Schedule sets the wall

A higher schedule means a thicker wall, a smaller bore and more weight per metre.

TL;DR

  • Pipe schedule is a wall-thickness designation; the higher the number, the thicker the wall.
  • OD is fixed for each NPS, so a thicker wall reduces the inside diameter.
  • From NPS 14 upward, the OD in inches equals the NPS: NPS 14 is 14.000 in (355.6 mm).
  • SCH 40 equals STD up to NPS 10; from NPS 12 STD stays at 9.53 mm while SCH 40 keeps growing.
  • SCH 80 equals XS up to NPS 8; from NPS 10 XS stays at 12.70 mm.
  • Carbon steel schedules come from ASME B36.10M; stainless 5S, 10S, 40S and 80S come from ASME B36.19M.
  • The schedule is chosen from a wall thickness calculation, then fixed for each size in the project's line class.

What Does Pipe Schedule Mean?

A pipe schedule is a number that designates wall thickness for a given nominal pipe size. For the same NPS, SCH 80 is thicker than SCH 40, and SCH 160 thicker still. The schedule is not a thickness in itself: SCH 40 is 3.38 mm at NPS 1 but 6.02 mm at NPS 4.

The key rule is that the outside diameter stays the same for every schedule of one NPS, so fittings, flanges and supports fit all of them. The wall grows inward. For NPS 1/8 to 12 the NPS is only a nominal label (NPS 4 is 4.500 in OD). From NPS 14 upward, the OD in inches equals the NPS, as the chart shows: NPS 14 = 14.000 in, NPS 24 = 24.000 in (610 mm).

NPS, NB and DN: Pipe Size Naming

NPS (nominal pipe size) is the inch designation used by ASME. DN (diamètre nominal) is the metric equivalent. NB (nominal bore) is the term used on many Indian drawings and in IS 1239, and is written in mm like DN. Indian and Gulf drawings often mix all three, so it pays to know the pairs. Up to NPS 3 the mapping has to be learned; from NPS 4 up, DN = 25 × NPS (Octal Steel).

How to Choose the Right Pipe Schedule

The schedule is the last step of a pressure design calculation, not a guess. For process piping to ASME B31.3, the minimum thickness follows paragraph 304.1.2 (Project Materials):

t = P × D / [2 (S E W + P Y)] + cP design pressure · D outside diameter · S allowable stress at design temperature · E joint efficiency · W weld strength reduction factor · Y coefficient · c corrosion allowance
  1. Design pressure and temperature from the process data or line list.
  2. Allowable stress for the pipe material at design temperature, from the code tables.
  3. Add the corrosion allowance set by the piping material specification.
  4. Allow for mill tolerance: ASTM A106 and A53 permit the wall to be 12.5% under nominal, so divide by 0.875.
  5. Round up to the next schedule in the chart. Example: NPS 6 needing 5.23 mm after tolerance gets SCH 40 (7.11 mm).

The full worked calculation is on our pipe wall thickness calculation page.

Pipe Schedules on Indian and Gulf Projects

India

Water, fire and utility lines in buildings often use IS 1239 (Part 1) steel tubes, classed Light, Medium and Heavy for 15 to 150 mm nominal bore, not ASME schedules (BIS). Refinery, petrochemical and power projects follow ASME B36.10M and B36.19M.

Gulf oil and gas

High design pressures in oil and gas service push the calculated wall up, so higher pressure classes often land on SCH 80, SCH 160 or XXS. Small-bore lines (typically NPS 2 and below) are usually specified as SCH 80 or SCH 160 for corrosion allowance and mechanical strength, because small connections are a major source of vibration fatigue failures (Project Materials).

On both, the piping line class (piping material specification) fixes the schedule for every size in that class, so designers read it from the spec rather than choosing per line. As an illustration, a class might call for SCH 160 on small-bore sizes, SCH 80 on mid sizes and SCH 40 above; the actual split depends on pressure, temperature, material and corrosion allowance.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the schedules of pipes?
ASME B36.10M lists SCH 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, 140 and 160, plus the weight classes STD, XS and XXS. ASME B36.19M adds the stainless schedules 5S, 10S, 40S and 80S. Not every schedule exists for every size.
How thick is 1 inch schedule 40 pipe?
3.38 mm (0.133 in), on an OD of 33.4 mm (1.315 in).
What is the meaning of schedule 40 and schedule 80 pipe?
They are wall-thickness designations. For the same NPS, schedule 80 has a thicker wall than schedule 40, so it handles higher pressure and weighs more. NPS 2 SCH 40 is 3.91 mm thick; SCH 80 is 5.54 mm.
Is schedule 40 the same as STD?
Only up to NPS 10. From NPS 12, STD is 9.53 mm, while SCH 40 is 10.31 mm at NPS 12 and thicker at larger sizes.
What is the difference between NPS and DN?
NPS is the inch-based nominal size used by ASME; DN is the metric nominal size. They describe the same pipe: NPS 4 = DN 100, NPS 1 = DN 25. Neither is an exact measured diameter.
Which standard defines pipe schedules?
ASME B36.10M for welded and seamless wrought steel pipe, and ASME B36.19M for stainless steel pipe.
