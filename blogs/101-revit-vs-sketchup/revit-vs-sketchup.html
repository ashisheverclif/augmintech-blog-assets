SketchUp is a fast, flexible 3D modeller: you push and pull faces to shape ideas quickly. Revit is BIM software: you build a model of real building elements, with model-based views and schedules alongside manually added annotations and details. SketchUp wins when you are exploring a design; Revit wins when you have to document, schedule and coordinate it. The tools can complement each other, but a project does not always need both. This guide compares them in depth, shows how they work together, and helps you decide which to learn first.

3D modelling · conceptSketchUpEdges and faces grouped into groups and components. Quick to learn, quick to model, runs on Windows and Mac. Drawings are made in LayOut, its companion app.
BIM · documentationRevitWalls, floors, doors, rooms and furniture as intelligent objects in one model. Model-based views and schedules stay connected; settings and annotations still need checking. Windows only.

TL;DR

  • SketchUp is faster for concept design, massing and client 3D; Revit is slower to start but far stronger for drawing sets, schedules and teamwork.
  • SketchUp models geometry; Revit models building elements with data, which is why Revit can produce live schedules and coordinated drawings.
  • SketchUp Pro costs US$399 a year and Revit US$3,005 at 2026 US list prices; students pay US$55 a year for SketchUp and nothing for Revit through Autodesk's education plan.
  • They work together: SketchUp models can be linked into Revit as a reference, and SketchUp can import and export IFC.
  • Choose your first tool by the required deliverable rather than assuming everybody needs the same learning order.

Modelling: Faces vs Building Elements

In SketchUp, a wall usually starts as edges and faces organised into a group or component. Naming, classification, attributes and extensions can add information, and some components can cut openings. However, ordinary geometry does not acquire Revit-style wall layers, level constraints and hosted-element relationships just because it looks like a wall. This flexibility makes SketchUp useful for exploring forms and bespoke interiors.

In Revit, a wall is an object of a wall type, such as "230 mm brick with 12 mm plaster both sides". It knows its height, its base and top levels, its layers and materials, and it hosts the doors and windows placed in it. Change the wall type once and every wall of that type changes; move a level and the walls attached to it follow. That structure takes longer to set up, and supports coordinated outputs. Constraints, annotations and model information still need checking.

SketchUp gives you

  • Freedom to model any form, in any order
  • Groups and components for repetition
  • Dynamic components for simple parametric objects
  • A huge free library of models in 3D Warehouse

Revit gives you

  • Building elements with types, layers and data
  • Hosted elements: doors and windows cut their walls
  • Levels that walls, floors and roofs attach to
  • Families with parameters for size, material and maker

Design Speed: Why SketchUp Wins Early

At concept stage you change your mind every hour. SketchUp suits that: push a block up two floors, carve a courtyard, rotate the plan, check the shadows at 4 pm, and show the client three options in an afternoon. Scenes save camera views and shadow settings, so presenting is quick.

Revit can do conceptual massing too, but it asks for decisions earlier: levels, wall types, which element is which. Many architects find that slows down free design. The usual answer is not to choose, but to sequence: design in SketchUp, then rebuild the chosen scheme in Revit once the form is settled.

Drawings: LayOut vs Revit Sheets

This is the biggest practical difference. SketchUp Pro includes LayOut, a separate app where you place views of your SketchUp model on sheets at a chosen scale, then add dimensions, text, title blocks and details. It works well for small projects, interiors and presentation sheets, and LayOut can reload the model when it changes.

LayOut is more capable than a static sheet editor: linked model references, associated dimensions and model-aware labels can update. You still need deliberate scene, section-plane and viewport management, and a workflow for reports and schedules. Revit integrates building-element data and documentation more deeply, which becomes valuable across a larger set. See Trimble’s reference-update guidance and dimension guidance.

In Revit, plans, sections and elevations can be views of the same model. Tags and schedules read selected parameters. Model changes propagate to affected model-based views, but manually drafted details, text notes, view settings and constraints still need review. Revit reduces repeated editing; it does not remove drawing responsibility.

Data and Schedules

SketchUp is not without data. Components can carry attributes, and SketchUp's Generate Report builds tables from the objects in a model, such as component quantities, tags and sizes, and downloads them as a CSV file for materials lists and estimates. That is useful for counting furniture or panels.

Revit goes further. Its schedules are live views inside the model: door, window, room, furniture and material takeoff schedules update the moment the model changes, can be placed on sheets, and can be edited from the schedule itself. On a project with hundreds of doors or rooms, that is the difference between a report you regenerate and a live view of the information you have actually modelled. A live schedule can still be wrong when categories, parameters, phases or filters are wrong.

Worked Example: One Apartment, One Late Design Change

Use a fictional apartment with a 4,800 × 3,600 mm living-dining room, a bedroom and a compact kitchen. Keep the same brief in both tools: seating for four, a dining table, built-in storage and a clear route to the balcony. These are exercise dimensions, not code-compliant design recommendations.

1. Develop two layouts

In SketchUp, draw accurately, keep raw geometry organised inside groups, use components for repeated furniture and save a scene for each option. In Revit, establish levels and wall types, place appropriately categorised furniture families and use separate design options or separate exercise files without mixing alternatives. Compare the same camera and plan orientation.

2. Produce the same review package

Submit a furniture plan, an internal elevation of the storage wall, a section through the room and a furniture list. In SketchUp, prepare the scenes and section cuts before referencing them in LayOut. In Revit, create the views and schedule with the required category and fields. In either tool, place them on a readable sheet and export a PDF.

3. Move a door and widen the storage

Now shift the bedroom door 300 mm and increase the cabinet width from 1,200 to 1,500 mm. Check the swing, furniture access and remaining circulation. In SketchUp, edit the correct group or component and update the LayOut reference and relevant report. In Revit, check whether the width is a type or instance parameter before editing: a type change may alter every instance of that type.

4. Prove the revision is coordinated

Review the plan, elevation, section, dimensions, schedule and perspective. Look for stale text notes, cropped geometry and missing schedule rows. Re-export the PDF and record the revision. The better workflow is the one that produces the required checked outputs efficiently in your project context, not the one that makes the first attractive view fastest.

Portfolio evidence: show the brief, one rejected option, the final plan, a developed interior elevation, a schedule and the before/after revision. Add your role, software versions and what you checked. This gives a reviewer evidence of design thinking and documentation skill.

A Learning Path With Measurable Outputs

Start with one room rather than an entire complex building. First demonstrate accurate geometry and organised objects. Then produce a plan and elevation at a consistent scale. Next add furniture data, materials and a presentation view. Finally make a design revision and check every affected output. Move forward when you can repeat the task independently, not simply when you have watched the lesson.

For a SketchUp-focused path, make scenes, section cuts, components and LayOut part of the first project. For a Revit-focused path, include types, constraints, view templates, tags and schedules. Learn a renderer after the model and drawing workflow are reliable. There is no universal number of days that makes a student job-ready.

Using SketchUp and Revit Together

A combined workflow can use both, provided the extra handover serves a clear purpose. SketchUp's own help describes three ways to bring a SketchUp model into Revit, and the reverse route exists too:

Link the SKPInsert it with Link CAD. It stays external, so you can update it in SketchUp and reload it in Revit, and use it as a reference to model native walls, floors and roofs.
In-place componentBring the SKP inside an in-place family, for one-off objects such as a sculptural canopy, with SketchUp tags controlling visibility.
IFCExport IFC from SketchUp and open it in Revit. Only classified groups and components that are solid come through, as in-place components.
Revit to SketchUpSketchUp Studio (and the Pro Advanced Workflows plan) includes a Revit importer, Windows only, for taking Revit models into SketchUp for design studies or visualisation.

Two practical tips from SketchUp's help: check supported SKP versions for your Revit release; older Revit releases may need an older SKP format, and remember that SketchUp walls do not become native Revit walls. Use the linked model as a guide and rebuild walls, floors and roofs in Revit. Also note that Autodesk's comparison lists SketchUp import as one of the features Revit LT does not include.

Rendering and Client Presentations

Both can form part of an architectural rendering workflow. Choose the renderer only after checking its supported host release, operating system, GPU requirements and licence. Do not assume that every plugin supports every new Revit or SketchUp release immediately. Good materials, lighting, camera composition and model quality matter more than the host application name. For interior presentations, show a plan alongside the render so readers can judge furniture scale and circulation.

Moving From SketchUp to Revit: 6 Habits to Change

  1. Modelling everything as generic geometry. In Revit, use the right tool: Wall, Floor, Roof, Door. Generic-model families can be scheduled in their category, but they do not automatically behave like walls, doors or floors. Choose the category and parameters to match the required output.
  2. Ignoring levels. Set appropriate base levels, top constraints and offsets. A level change affects constrained elements; unconnected heights and manual geometry require separate checking.
  3. Drawing thickness by hand. Wall and floor thickness comes from their types. Create the type once, then reuse it.
  4. Hunting for free models. 3D Warehouse habits bring heavy, wrongly categorised objects. Use clean Revit families in the right category.
  5. Making drawings at the end. In Revit, set up the required views and sheets early, then review their content as the model develops.
  6. Using tags like SketchUp tags. In Revit, visibility is controlled per view by category and view templates, not by turning tags on and off.

Version and File Watch-outs

  • Revit files only open forwards. A model saved in a newer Revit release cannot be opened in an earlier one, so agree one Revit version for the team.
  • Check SKP compatibility before export. For older Revit releases, Trimble suggests considering an older SketchUp file format. Confirm the actual supported import versions and test a small file first.
  • IFC is not perfect. SketchUp's help warns that some entities may not export, and recommends checking the file straight after export.
  • Watch file size. Heavy 3D Warehouse models slow down both SketchUp and any Revit model they are linked into.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Revit better than SketchUp?
For drawing sets, schedules and teamwork on larger projects, yes. For concept design, quick 3D and small projects, SketchUp is usually faster. They do different jobs, and many offices use both.
Can SketchUp do BIM?
Partly. SketchUp can classify objects and import and export IFC, and Generate Report can list component data. But it does not have Revit's building elements, live schedules or worksharing, so it is not usually used as the main BIM tool on large projects.
Can I make working drawings in SketchUp?
Yes, with LayOut, which comes with SketchUp Pro. It suits small projects and interiors. For larger drawing sets with many sheets and schedules, Revit is much more efficient.
Can I import a SketchUp model into Revit?
Yes. You can link it with Link CAD, place it in an in-place family, or export IFC from SketchUp and open it in Revit. SketchUp walls do not become native Revit walls; use the model as a reference to build them. Revit LT does not include SketchUp import.
Should I learn SketchUp or Revit first?
Start with SketchUp for concept modelling and visualisation, or Revit for model-based documentation. Your course brief and target role should determine the order. If you already work in an office that uses Revit, start with Revit.
Does Revit run on a Mac?
Revit has no native macOS application. Verify support for your hardware and Revit release before planning a virtualised Windows setup. Current SketchUp releases support Apple silicon Macs.
How much do SketchUp and Revit cost?
At 2026 US list prices, SketchUp Pro is US$399 a year and Studio US$859; students pay US$55. Revit is US$3,005 a year, and students can use it free for learning through Autodesk's education plan.
