Short answer

A cable tray is an open support system that carries and routes cables through a building or plant. Five types cover most projects: ladder, perforated, solid bottom, wire mesh and channel. This guide helps you pick the right type, material and size, not shop for one.

TL;DR

  • Ladder trays suit heavy power cables and long spans; wire mesh suits data and low-voltage cables.
  • Material follows the environment: pre-galvanised indoors, hot-dip galvanised outdoors, stainless 316 or GRP on the coast or in chemical plants.
  • Size by fill and load: NEC Article 392 limits fill, and the maker's load table, tested to IEC 61537, sets the support spacing.
  • Gulf coastal sites corrode zinc fast, which pushes specifications toward hot-dip galvanised, stainless or GRP trays.

What Is a Cable Tray?

A cable tray is a rigid structural system (open or ventilated, with side rails) that supports insulated cables between equipment. It is not a raceway: cables lie in it rather than being pulled through it. Here is how it compares with the two alternatives:

Cable trayOpen support. Easy to add or replace cables, good heat dissipation, carries many cables over long runs. Used for main power, control and data routes.
Cable trunkingEnclosed metal or PVC box with a removable lid. Protects cables from dust and touch. Used for final circuits and wiring in finished areas.
ConduitClosed tube; cables are pulled through. Best physical protection, but hard to modify. Used for individual circuits, concealed and exposed wiring.

5 Types of Cable Tray

1. Ladder cable tray

Two side rails joined by rungs. Without covers, it gives the maximum free flow of air across the cables, and is used on intermediate to long support spans of 12 to 30 ft (about 3.7 to 9 m).

Best for: MV and main LV power cables, transformer and switchgear rooms, plant pipe racks

2. Perforated cable tray

A sheet-steel base with ventilation holes (also called ventilated trough). Moderate ventilation with more continuous cable support, on spans of 5 to 12 ft (about 1.5 to 3.7 m).

Best for: LV power and control cables in buildings

3. Solid bottom cable tray

A continuous, non-ventilated base that fully supports delicate cables and, with a cover, protects them. Used for minimal heat-generating electrical or telecom cables on spans of 5 to 12 ft.

Best for: instrumentation, telecom and cables needing protection or EMI shielding

4. Wire mesh (basket) cable tray

A light grid of welded steel wire, easy to cut and bend on site. Used mainly for telecom and fibre optic cables on short spans of 4 to 8 ft (about 1.2 to 2.4 m).

Best for: data, ICT and low-current systems above ceilings and in data centres

5. Channel cable tray

A narrow single channel for a small number of cables where conduit is undesirable, on short to medium spans of 5 to 10 ft (about 1.5 to 3 m).

Best for: drops to equipment, a few control or instrument cables

Descriptions and spans from an IDC technical reference; typical cables from Engalaxy. Spans are typical ranges; the maker's load table decides the actual spacing.

Cable Tray Sizing and Fill

Pick the width for fill and future cables, and the depth (side rail height) to contain the cable bundle. In the US, cable fill follows NEC Article 392 (NFPA 70). The main rules, as set out in Eaton B-Line's cable tray manual:

  • Multiconductor cables 4/0 AWG and largerSingle layer; the sum of the cable diameters must not exceed the tray's inside width (Legrand NEC fill calculator).
  • Smaller multiconductor cablesTotal cross-sectional area must not exceed the allowable fill area for that tray width in the NEC table (for example, 7.0 in² in the manual's 6 in wide example).
  • Control and signal cables onlyLadder or ventilated trough trays up to 6 in deep can be filled to 50% of their area; deeper trays are calculated as 6 in deep.
  • Solid bottom traysLower fill: about 30% for mixed cables, and only 90% of the width for 4/0 and larger.
Example: control cables, 300 mm × 100 mm ladder tray → 30,000 mm² × 50% = 15,000 mm² of cable area

Rules from Eaton (Cooper) B-Line Cable Tray Manual, which cites NEC 392.9 (renumbered 392.22 in recent editions). The 2026 NEC is current; check the edition your authority applies. IEC 61537:2023 covers tray strength and electrical continuity tests rather than fill, so on IEC projects fill and spare capacity come from the project specification.

Support Spacing and Installation

Support spacing comes from the maker's load table, tested to IEC 61537 (2023 edition). The test loads trays and ladders on spans of up to 6 m and records deflection to set a safe working load (SWL) for each span (Sparks Electrical News). Load capacity falls fast as the span grows.   • Check the load: add up the cable weights in kg/m (or N/m), plus any allowance the spec requires, and pick a span the table allows.
  • Support near joints and fittings: place supports close to splices, bends and tees, as the maker's instructions show.
  • Respect bend radius: use bends with an inside radius that suits the largest cable's minimum bending radius.
  • Earth continuity: IEC 61537 tests the electrical continuity of tray systems; bond across joints with the maker's links where the tray is earthed.
  • Leave access: keep space above and beside trays to pull in cables later.

Modelling Cable Trays in Revit

In Revit, cable tray is drawn from Systems › Electrical › Cable Tray. From the Type Selector you choose a cable tray type, with or without fittings, then set the width, height, offset and bend radius on the ribbon; bends are added automatically as you draw (Autodesk Revit Help).

  • Set up types for ladder and channel trays, with the right fittings (bends, tees, crosses, reducers) loaded.
  • Route in coordination zones: agree tray heights with HVAC and plumbing so every tray keeps access and clearance for pulling cables.
  • Check clashes with Collaborate › Coordinate › Interference Check against ducts, pipes and structure (Autodesk Help).
  • Schedule quantities: cable tray and fitting schedules give lengths and counts for the BOQ.

Cable Trays on Gulf Projects

Gulf sites combine heat, humidity and airborne salt, and coastal sites corrode zinc far faster than inland ones. A corrosion study at five sites in Abu Dhabi measured:

15 µm/yrZinc corrosion rate on the coast
0.6 µm/yrZinc corrosion rate inland
56 µm/yrSteel corrosion rate on the coast (10 inland)

Ganther et al. (2011), "Towards the development of a corrosion map for Abu Dhabi", Materials and Corrosion. The authors link the high rates to salt build-up, made worse by very low rainfall. A study at Jubail, Saudi Arabia, also found the Gulf splash zone "much more corrosive to mild and galvanized steel than the other parts of the world" (Saricimen et al., 2010).

Put those numbers side by side with coating thickness. At 15 µm of zinc lost per year, a 45 µm hot-dip galvanised tray coating would be gone in roughly three years on an exposed coastal site (a simple division that ignores shelter and local variation), and a 13 µm pre-galvanised coat in under a year. That is the case for specifying hot-dip galvanised trays as a minimum outdoors, and stainless steel 316L or GRP on exposed coastal, offshore and chemical sites. Indoors, in dry air-conditioned spaces, pre-galvanised remains the standard finish.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the types of cable tray?
The five main types are ladder, perforated (ventilated trough), solid bottom, wire mesh (basket) and channel cable trays. They are made in pre-galvanised or hot-dip galvanised steel, stainless steel, aluminium or FRP/GRP.
Which cable tray is best for power cables?
Ladder cable tray. It gives the most ventilation, which helps cables carry their rated current, and it supports heavy cables over long spans. Perforated tray suits smaller LV power and control cables.
What is the difference between a cable tray and cable trunking?
A cable tray is open, so cables are easy to add and stay cool; it is used for main cable routes. Trunking is an enclosed box with a lid, protecting cables in finished areas and for final circuits.
What is the maximum fill for a cable tray?
Under NEC Article 392, control and signal cables may fill 50% of a ladder or ventilated trough tray's area (up to 6 in deep). Multiconductor power cables 4/0 and larger go in a single layer with diameters adding up to no more than the tray width. On IEC projects, fill and spare capacity come from the project specification.
What is the support spacing for a cable tray?
It depends on the tray and the cable load, so take it from the maker's load table tested to IEC 61537. Typical spans are 4 to 8 ft for wire mesh, 5 to 12 ft for perforated and solid bottom trays, and 12 to 30 ft for ladders.
