Short answer

ASME B16.5 covers six main flange types: weld neck, slip-on, socket weld, threaded, lap joint and blind. Each suits a different pressure, size and service. This guide shows when to use each one, how the facings (RF, FF, RTJ) differ, and how to read a flange pressure class.

If you are filling in a line class or a flange datasheet, you need to choose four things: the flange type, the facing, the pressure class and the material. Pictures of flanges will not help you choose. The selection logic will.

TL;DR

  • A flanged joint is two flanges, a gasket and a set of bolts, used where a line must be opened for maintenance or connected to equipment.
  • ASME B16.5 covers NPS 1/2 to 24; larger flanges (NPS 26 to 60) are in ASME B16.47.
  • Weld neck flanges are the choice for high pressure and temperature, thanks to their tapered hub and butt weld.
  • Slip-on flanges are easier to fit but have about two-thirds of a weld neck's strength.
  • Socket weld and threaded flanges are for small bore; threaded is used where welding is not possible.
  • Lap joint flanges save cost on alloy lines, and blind flanges close a line end.
  • Raised face (RF) is the most common facing; RTJ is used at Class 600 and above or above 427 °C.
  • A pressure class is not a pressure in psi: Class 150 carbon steel is rated 19.6 bar at ambient and less as temperature rises.

What Is a Flange in Piping?

A flange is a ring or plate welded, screwed or fitted to the end of a pipe so it can be bolted to another flange, a valve or a piece of equipment. A flanged joint has three parts: two flanges, a gasket between them, and stud bolts with nuts that clamp the gasket.

MaintenanceA joint can be opened to remove a valve, pump or spool without cutting pipe.
EquipmentPumps, vessels, exchangers and most valves come with flanged nozzles.
IsolationBlinds and spectacle blinds can be inserted between flanges for positive isolation.

The trade-off is that every flanged joint is a potential leak path, adds weight and cost, and needs bolt tightening. Lines that never need opening are usually butt-welded instead.

6 Main Flange Types

The six types differ in how they attach to the pipe. That one difference drives their strength, cost and where they are allowed. The drawings below show the upper half of each in cross-section, with pipe entering from the left.

Weld Neck, Slip-On and Socket Weld Flanges

Weld neck (WN)

butt weldall classes

A long tapered hub is butt-welded to the pipe, so stress passes smoothly from pipe to flange. The hub reinforcement suits high-pressure, sub-zero and high-temperature service, and critical welds can be fully radiographed (The Process Piping).

Use for: high pressure or temperature, cyclic or critical service, most process lines.

Slip-on (SO)

2 fillet weldslower strength

The pipe slides into the flange and is fillet-welded inside and outside. It is easier to align than a weld neck, but its strength is "of the order of two-thirds that of Welding Neck flanges" (The Process Piping).

Use for: low-pressure utility lines such as cooling water, where the spec allows it.

Socket weld (SW)

1 fillet weldsmall bore

The pipe sits in a socket and is fillet-welded on the outside, with a small gap left at the bottom. It is for small-bore lines only and not recommended for severe service; the gap can trap fluid and is a corrosion risk.

Use for: small-bore process and utility lines (the line class sets the size limit).

Threaded, Lap Joint and Blind Flanges

Threaded (screwed)

no weldlow P and T

Screws onto a threaded pipe end. Used "on pipe lines where welding cannot be carried out" (The Process Piping), and generally for relatively low pressure and temperature, such as water and compressed air (Tameson).

Use for: small utility lines, galvanised pipe, and areas where hot work is not permitted.

Lap joint (with stub end)

loose flangealloy saving

A stub end is butt-welded to the pipe, and a loose backing flange slides over it. Only the stub end touches the fluid, so on an expensive alloy line the backing flange can be carbon steel. The loose flange also rotates freely, which makes bolt-hole alignment easy.

Use for: stainless or alloy lines, and joints that are dismantled often.

Blind

no boreline end

A solid plate with no bore, used "to blank off the ends of piping" (The Process Piping), valves and vessel nozzles. It carries the full pressure on its face, so it is thick.

Use for: line ends, future tie-ins, test points and nozzle closures.

Special Flanges

  • Orifice flangeA weld neck pair with pressure tappings, holding an orifice plate for flow measurement (ASME B16.36).
  • Reducing flangeA flange whose bore is for a smaller pipe than its nominal size, used to change size at a flanged joint.
  • Long weld neckA weld neck with an extended hub, often used as a vessel or column nozzle.
  • Spectacle blindA blind and a ring spacer joined by a tie bar, rotated between flanges for "absolute positive isolation"; ASME B16.48, NPS 1/2 to 24, Class 150 to 2500 (Project Materials).

Flange Facing Types: RF, FF and RTJ

The facing is the sealing surface where the gasket sits. What is FF and RF in flanges? RF (raised face) has a raised ring around the bore that carries the gasket; FF (flat face) is flat across the whole face and uses a full-face gasket.

Never bolt a raised face steel flange to a flat-faced gray cast iron flange. The raised ring creates a bending load that can crack the cast iron. Match it with a flat face flange and a full-face gasket instead (Industrial Monitor Direct).

Class 150 vs Class 300: at ambient, Class 300 Group 1.1 is rated 51.1 bar against 19.6 bar for Class 150, and the gap widens with temperature (39.8 vs 10.2 bar at 300 °C). Class 300 flanges are also thicker, with larger bolt circles, so the two do not mate.

Flanges in Gulf Oil and Gas Projects

High pressure: Class 600+ with RTJ

Wellhead, gas gathering and injection lines run at high pressures, which push the calculated class to 600, 900, 1500 or 2500. At these classes, and above 427 °C, RTJ facings with metal ring gaskets are the usual choice because they hold a seal where flat gaskets would not (The Process Piping).

Sour service: NACE MR0175 / ISO 15156

Where the gas contains H₂S, flange, bolting and ring gasket materials must resist sulphide stress cracking under NACE MR0175 / ISO 15156 for production service, or NACE MR0103 for refineries (TWI).

On a Gulf EPC project, the piping material specification fixes flange type, facing, class, material and gasket for each line class. Datasheets then add the sour-service, hardness and testing requirements that the operator's specification calls for.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main types of flanges?
The six main types under ASME B16.5 are weld neck, slip-on, socket weld, threaded, lap joint and blind. Special flanges include orifice, reducing and long weld neck flanges, and spectacle blinds.
What is the difference between class 150 and class 300 flanges?
Class 300 flanges are rated for higher pressure and are thicker, with a larger bolt circle. For carbon steel (Group 1.1) at ambient temperature, Class 150 is rated 19.6 bar and Class 300 is 51.1 bar. They are not interchangeable.
What is FF and RF in flanges?
FF is flat face: the sealing surface is flat across the whole face and uses a full-face gasket. RF is raised face: a raised ring around the bore carries a ring gasket. RF is the most common facing in process piping; FF is used to mate with cast iron or flat-faced equipment.
What are the types of flange faces?
Raised face (RF), flat face (FF), ring type joint (RTJ), tongue and groove (T&G), and male and female (M&F).
Which flange is used for high pressure?
A weld neck flange, usually with an RTJ facing at Class 600 and above. Its tapered hub and full-penetration butt weld carry high pressure and temperature, and the weld can be fully radiographed.
What is the difference between ASME B16.5 and B16.47?
ASME B16.5 covers flanges from NPS 1/2 to 24 in Classes 150 to 2500. ASME B16.47 covers large-diameter flanges from NPS 26 to 60 in Classes 75 to 900, in two non-interchangeable series: Series A (formerly MSS SP-44) and Series B (formerly API 605).
