Revit
Revit lets an interior designer build one model of rooms, finishes, furniture and ceilings, then pull every drawing and schedule from it: furniture plan, finish plan, reflected ceiling plan, interior elevations and room finish schedule. Move a sofa or change a floor finish once and every view and schedule updates. The trade-offs are a steeper learning curve and a higher price than AutoCAD or SketchUp. This guide walks through the full workflow, tool by tool, and is honest about where Revit is the wrong choice.

TL;DR

  • Rooms are the backbone: each room carries its name, number, area and floor, wall, ceiling and base finishes, which drive the room finish schedule.
  • Furniture and casework are loadable families, so they can be counted and scheduled automatically.
  • Ceilings are modelled in ceiling plan views, which gives you the reflected ceiling plan without redrawing.
  • Design options let you show a client two or three layouts in one file without copying the project.
  • Revit pays off on larger, repeating or BIM-required jobs; for a single small flat, AutoCAD plus SketchUp is often quicker.

The Workflow: Furniture Layout to Drawing Set in 8 Steps

This is the order that works on a typical apartment or office fit-out. It is Augmintech's recommended sequence, not a fixed rule.

Follow one living room from an empty shell to a coordinated drawing sheet. The window, doorway and furniture positions stay consistent, making each addition easy to compare. These are illustrative teaching views, not captures from Revit.

  1. Set up the shellLink or model the architect's walls, doors, windows and levels. If you receive DWG plans, link them and trace walls on top.
  2. Place roomsAdd a room in every enclosed space, then name and number it. Areas calculate automatically.
  3. Lay out furnitureLoad furniture families and place them. Use families with real dimensions so clearances are true.
  4. Add casework and joineryPlace wardrobes, vanities, kitchen units and built-in storage as casework families.
  5. Apply finishesSet floor and wall types, and use Split Face and Paint for feature walls and inlays.
  6. Model ceilingsWork in ceiling plan views to add false ceilings, bulkheads and light fittings.
  7. Create viewsCut interior elevations for each room, colour-fill plans by finish and set up schedules.
  8. Place on sheetsDrop views and schedules onto titled sheets, tag, dimension and issue.

Set Up Before You Draw: Your Interior Template

The biggest time-saver in Revit interiors happens before the first project: a project template that already holds your standards. Every new project then starts with the right finishes, tags and sheets instead of rebuilding them.

The core of a good template is a set of view templates. Autodesk describes a view template as a way to apply many visibility and graphic settings to a view at once; when several views share one template they look the same, and editing the template updates every view that uses it. For interiors, that means your furniture plans, finish plans and ceiling plans always print with the same line weights, scales and visible categories.

Working With the Architect's Revit Model

On most projects, the architect owns the building and you own the interior. The usual way to keep both in step is to link the architect's Revit model into your own interior model rather than working inside their file.

  • Link, do not copy. A linked model updates when the architect issues a new version, so your plans always sit on their latest walls.
  • Make the link room-bounding. By default, linked walls do not bound rooms. Autodesk's help explains how to switch on the linked model's Room Bounding type property, after which you can place rooms in your model using the architect's walls.
  • Agree the split. Decide in writing who models partitions, doors, ceilings and fixed joinery, so nothing is modelled twice or not at all.
  • Agree the Revit version. Revit models cannot be opened in an earlier release, so every team member must work on the same version.

Rooms: The Data Behind Every Schedule

A Revit room is an invisible element that fills an enclosed space and stores information about it. Autodesk's help describes using a room finish schedule to list the flooring, wall covering, ceiling and trim for each room, and to estimate the cost and quantity of materials. You can also add your own room parameters, such as décor style, and sort or filter the schedule by them.

Room areas are measured to the wall finish by default. Autodesk's help lists four boundary options for the whole project: at wall finish, wall centre, wall core layer or wall core centre.

Room area is not carpet area. In India, RERA's carpet area includes the internal partition walls of a flat. A Revit room measured at wall finish stops at the face of each wall, so adding up room areas gives a smaller figure than RERA carpet area. For sale or legal figures, use a separate area plan and the RERA definition, not a total of room areas.

Worked Example: A Room Finish Code Is Not a Material Takeoff

Suppose Bedroom 01 specifies porcelain tile. Enter FIN-T01 in its Floor Finish room parameter and add that field to the room schedule. This records the specification. It does not assign tile to the floor element, create a finish layer or calculate tile procurement automatically.

Next, assign the intended material and thickness to the appropriate modelled floor type, checking its boundaries against the design. Use a material takeoff when you need quantities from those model elements. Keep a separate finish-code legend linking FIN-T01 to the product description and installation specification. Reconcile the room schedule, finish plan and model before issuing them.

For example, a modelled finish area of 20 m² with an assumed 10% allowance gives 22 m² before pack rounding. This is arithmetic for a learning example, not a universal wastage rate. Confirm tile size, pattern, cuts, pack coverage and supplier advice. Do not label net model area as an order quantity.

Floors, Walls and Ceilings

Floors and walls. Floors and walls are system families built in layers, so a floor type can hold the screed and the tile as separate layers with their own materials. For a feature wall or a floor inlay, Revit's Split Face tool divides one face of an element into sections without changing its structure, and the Paint tool then applies a different material to each section.

Ceilings. Ceilings are placed in a ceiling plan view. Click inside a closed loop of walls and Revit places a ceiling within them automatically, or sketch your own boundary for bulkheads and coves. Because the ceiling, its height and its light fittings are in the model, the reflected ceiling plan is just a view of it.

Colour schemes. A colour scheme fills rooms in a plan or section by the value of a parameter, such as floor finish or department, and places a matching legend. One colour-filled plan can replace a hand-drawn finish key.

Lighting and the Reflected Ceiling Plan

For interior designers, the reflected ceiling plan (RCP) is often the most coordinated drawing in the set, because ceilings, lights, AC grilles, sprinklers and speakers all meet there. In Revit, it is simply a ceiling plan view of the model, so it stays in step with the floor plan.

  • Ceiling types and heights. Model each ceiling with its type (gypsum, grid, wood slat) and height offset; tag both on the RCP so the contractor sees levels without a separate section.
  • Bulkheads and coves. Sketch separate ceiling boundaries at different heights, or model the drop as its own ceiling or wall, so sections show it correctly.
  • Light fittings as families. Downlights, linear profiles and pendants are lighting fixture families. Many are hosted on a ceiling face, so they move with the ceiling, and a lighting fixture schedule counts them by type.
  • Coordinate with MEP. Link the MEP model and check that lights do not clash with ducts, diffusers or sprinklers before issuing the RCP.
  • Lighting layers. Show ambient, task and accent lighting with different fixture types and a legend, so the client and the electrical engineer read the same intent.

Furniture and Casework Families

Furniture, casework and lighting fixtures in Revit are loadable families: separate files you load into the project and place with the Component tool. Each family has types, such as a sofa in two-, three- and four-seat sizes, and parameters for size, material and manufacturer. Because every chair is a counted object, a furniture schedule tells you how many of each type you have, which helps with procurement.

You can use the families that come with Revit, download manufacturers' families, or build your own in the Family Editor. Building good families takes time, so most offices keep a small, checked library rather than downloading everything.

Before any downloaded family goes into your library, check it:

Correct category. A wardrobe saved as Generic Model will not appear in your casework schedule.
Real dimensions. Measure it against the supplier's catalogue; clearances are only true if sizes are.
Sensible detail. Simple 2D symbols in plan, more detail in 3D. Over-modelled families slow the whole project down.
File size. A single chair that is several megabytes is a warning sign.
Useful parameters. Manufacturer, model, finish and a product code, so the furniture schedule is ready for procurement.
Consistent names. One naming pattern across the library, such as Category_Type_Size, so anyone can find things.

Design Options: Show the Client Alternatives

Clients often want to compare two or three layouts. Revit's design options let you develop alternatives inside one project file; Autodesk's help gives "exploring room or furniture layouts" as an example. You group alternatives into an option set, work on each one, and show them side by side on a sheet. When the client chooses, you accept that option into the main model and the others are removed.

Joinery details

Wardrobes, vanities, TV units and kitchens need more than a plan symbol. A practical Revit approach:

  • Model the joinery as casework families with parameters for width, height, depth, shutter type and finish, so plans, elevations and schedules agree.
  • Cut callouts and sections from the interior elevations at 1:10 or 1:5 for shutter, skirting and countertop details.
  • Add 2D detail components for hardware, edge banding and fixing details; modelling every hinge in 3D only slows the model.
  • Leave shop drawings to the fabricator, but give them dimensioned elevations, sections and a finish schedule they can build from.

Quantities: Material Takeoffs for Tiles, Paint and Panelling

Room finish schedules tell you what goes where; material takeoff schedules help with how much. Autodesk's help describes a material takeoff schedule as listing details such as material type, quantity and cost from the model. For an interior designer, that means you can schedule the area of each wall paint, floor tile or wall panel material and get a first estimate straight from the model.

Treat these numbers as a starting point, not a final bill of quantities. Add wastage, check how tiles are laid and how openings are counted, and have a quantity surveyor or contractor confirm them for tenders. The value is that when the layout changes, the takeoff changes with it.

Is Revit Right for Your Interior Work?

Where Revit helps

  • Projects with many repeating units, such as hotels, offices or housing
  • Late changes that would mean redrawing many sheets
  • Schedules and quantities for procurement
  • Projects where the architect and MEP teams already work in BIM

Where it slows you down

  • Early concept sketching, which is quicker in SketchUp
  • Custom furniture that needs new families
  • Small one-off jobs where AutoCAD drawings are enough
  • Cost: US$3,005 a year at the 2026 US list price

Many interior offices use both: SketchUp or AutoCAD for concept work and small jobs, and Revit for larger projects and the final drawing set. The AutoCAD Revit LT Suite, at US$680 a year, is cheaper, but check Autodesk's comparison first: Revit LT does not include view filters, in-place modelling, worksharing, in-product rendering or Dynamo, and interior work uses several of these often. For most interior teams, full Revit is the realistic choice. Students can use Revit free for learning through Autodesk's education plan.

Tip: do not start your first Revit project on a live client deadline. Rebuild a finished project in Revit first, so you learn the tools without time pressure.

A 6-Week Plan to Learn Revit for Interiors

If you already work in AutoCAD or SketchUp, this plan takes you to a complete interior set on one real project. Spend around an hour a day, and use a flat you have already designed so you can focus on the tool, not the design. This is Augmintech's suggested sequence, not a fixed syllabus.

  1. Week 1: Shell and roomsLevels, walls, doors and windows; link a DWG and trace it; place and tag rooms.
  2. Week 2: Furniture and caseworkLoad and place families; build one simple wardrobe family with width and finish parameters.
  3. Week 3: FinishesFloor and wall types, Split Face and Paint, a material library and a colour-filled finish plan.
  4. Week 4: Ceilings and lightingCeilings at different heights, a bulkhead, light fittings and a tagged RCP.
  5. Week 5: Elevations and detailsInterior elevations for each room, joinery callouts and sections, dimensions and tags.
  6. Week 6: Schedules and sheetsRoom finish, furniture and material takeoff schedules; view templates; a titled sheet set exported to PDF and DWG.

Learning alone works, but feedback speeds it up. A structured course is one way to get it; Augmintech's PG Program in BIM Architecture is one option to compare.

7 Common Mistakes Interior Designers Make in Revit

  1. Rooms that do not close. A gap between walls, or a linked model that is not room-bounding, leaves a room unplaced or merged with its neighbour. Use room separation lines for open-plan zones.
  2. Furniture in the wrong category. Families saved as Generic Model drop out of furniture schedules and visibility settings.
  3. Drawing finishes as lines. Hatches drawn on top of a plan do not appear in elevations or takeoffs. Use floor types, Split Face and Paint, or finish walls.
  4. Typing finish names by hand in every room. Typos create separate rows in the schedule. Use a key schedule or a fixed list of finish names.
  5. Too much detail in families. Heavy furniture models slow down every view. Keep geometry simple and detailed only where it is seen.
  6. No view templates. Without them, each sheet prints differently and every change means editing views one by one.
  7. Skipping the architect's coordination. Ceilings and lights modelled without checking the MEP model clash on site. Link and check before issuing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Revit good for interior design?
Yes, especially for larger or repeating projects. It produces furniture plans, reflected ceiling plans, interior elevations and finish schedules from one model, so changes update everywhere. For quick concepts and small jobs, SketchUp or AutoCAD is often faster.
Can Revit make a room finish schedule?
Yes. Rooms store floor, wall, ceiling and trim finishes, and a room schedule lists them for every room. You can add your own fields and sort or filter by them.
Where do I get Revit furniture families?
Revit includes a library of families, many manufacturers publish their own, and you can build custom pieces in the Family Editor. Check downloaded families for size, file weight and correct category before using them.
Should I learn SketchUp or Revit for interiors?
Learn SketchUp first for fast 3D design, then Revit when you need coordinated drawing sets and schedules. Many designers use both.
Can I calculate tile and paint quantities in Revit?
Yes, as a first estimate. A material takeoff schedule lists material quantities from the model, such as the area of each paint or tile. Add wastage and have quantities checked before tendering.
Should I work inside the architect's Revit file?
Usually not. Link the architect's model into your interior model and set the link as room-bounding, so you can place rooms against their walls while each team keeps its own file.
Can I use Revit LT for interior design?
You can, but Revit LT lacks view filters, in-place modelling, worksharing, in-product rendering and Dynamo. For anything beyond small solo projects, full Revit is usually the better choice.
How long does it take to learn Revit for interiors?
It depends on practice time and prior CAD experience. Working on one real project, our suggested 6-week plan covers the full workflow from rooms to a complete sheet set.
How much does Revit cost?
At 2026 US list prices, Revit costs US$3,005 a year and the AutoCAD Revit LT Suite US$680 a year. Students and educators can use it free for learning through Autodesk's education plan.

Check an Interior Model Before Issuing Drawings

Use a compact bedroom-and-bathroom exercise to test the full workflow. Produce a furniture plan, finish plan, reflected ceiling plan, four internal elevations, a room finish schedule and one interior render. All should describe the same option and revision.

  • A room is not enclosed: inspect room-bounding elements, boundary gaps, phase and linked-model settings. Place separation lines deliberately and document why they are needed.
  • A ceiling light is missing from the RCP: check its host and height, the view’s range, category visibility and phase. Do not duplicate the fitting until you know why it is invisible.
  • A furniture family makes the file heavy: use a simpler family with the correct footprint and clearance envelope. Add highly detailed assets only where presentation needs them.
  • A wall moves but the ceiling does not follow: inspect the ceiling sketch and constraints. Model relationships are not a substitute for a coordinated review.
  • The rendered material looks wrong: check the appearance asset, texture scale, lighting and exposure. A material’s graphic pattern and rendered appearance are separate settings.

Finish with a PDF review at the intended print scale. Check dimension references, door and drawer clearance, joinery heights, ceiling access and the consistency of finish codes. Keep coordination checks with building services separate from aesthetic approval: a polished render cannot prove that a ceiling is buildable.
