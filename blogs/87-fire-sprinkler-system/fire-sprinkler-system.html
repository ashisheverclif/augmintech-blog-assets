Short answer

A fire sprinkler system is a network of water-filled (or air-filled) pipes with heat-sensitive sprinkler heads. Each head opens on its own when the heat at that head reaches its temperature rating, and sprays water on the fire below it.

This page compares the four system types, explains sprinkler heads and bulb colours, and summarises NFPA 13 hazard classes and coverage rules in plain words, with the Revit MEP workflow and the Indian, UAE and Saudi code picture at the end.

TL;DR

  • Sprinklers open individually: only heads heated to their rating discharge, not the whole system.
  • There are four system types: wet pipe, dry pipe, pre-action and deluge.
  • Bulb colour shows the temperature rating: orange 57 °C and red 68 °C are the most common.
  • NFPA 13 sorts occupancies into light, ordinary (groups 1 and 2) and extra hazard (groups 1 and 2).
  • A standard spray head covers up to 225 ft² (20.9 m²) in light hazard and 130 ft² (12.1 m²) in ordinary hazard, at up to 15 ft (4.6 m) spacing.
  • India, the UAE and Saudi Arabia all point to NFPA 13 or IS 15105 for design, with local codes deciding where sprinklers are required.

How a Fire Sprinkler System Works

  1. Closed heads. Each sprinkler is held shut by a liquid-filled glass bulb (or a fusible metal link).
  2. Heat opens one head. Hot gases from the fire heat the nearest head. When its bulb reaches its rating, it bursts and water flows onto the deflector, which spreads it in a spray pattern.
  3. Only nearby heads open. Heads further away stay below their rating, so they stay closed and water goes only where the heat is.
  4. The alarm sounds. Water flowing through the alarm valve station trips a flow switch and alarm, and the fire pump starts if pressure drops.
Common mythIn films, every sprinkler in a building goes off at once. In a standard wet pipe system that does not happen: each head responds only to heat at its own location. Only a deluge system opens all heads together.

Sprinkler Head Types

PendentHangs below the pipe; the most common head in finished spaces with false ceilings.
UprightSits on top of the pipe, spraying up at the deflector; used in exposed ceilings and required in unheated dry or pre-action areas.
SidewallMounted on a wall, throwing water across the room; used in corridors, hotel rooms and small spaces.
ConcealedA recessed head behind a flat cover plate that drops off at about 11 °C (20 °F) below the head's rating.

Hazard Classification under NFPA 13

NFPA 13 sets the design by occupancy hazard: how much there is to burn, and how fast it burns. Typical examples:

Light hazard
Low quantity and combustibility of contents: offices, classrooms, hospitals, churches, museums, residential.
Ordinary group 1
Low combustibility, moderate quantity: parking garages, bakeries, bottling and dairy plants, commercial kitchens without high-temperature fryers.
Ordinary group 2
Moderate to high quantity and combustibility: auto repair garages, machine shops, supermarkets, textile mills, woodworking shops.
Extra group 1
High quantity and combustibility, little or no flammable liquid: aircraft hangars, sawmills, plywood plants, rubber processing, foam upholstery.
Extra group 2
Moderate to substantial flammable or combustible liquids: asphalt roofing, dip tanks, oil quenching, solvent degreasing, lacquer dipping.

Examples from an NFPA 13 occupancy classification summary. The designer classifies each area; one building can contain several hazard classes.

In plain words: sprinklers must be at least 6 ft (1.8 m) apart so one head's spray does not cool and delay its neighbour, no further from a wall than half the maximum spacing, and at least 4 in (102 mm) from a wall. Extended coverage heads are listed for larger areas, but only within their own listing.

Sprinkler Requirements in India and the Gulf

India: NBC 2016 Part 4

  • Table 7 sets where sprinklers are needed, by occupancy and building height
  • Design and installation to IS 15105
  • NBCS 2026 now published

UAE: Fire and Life Safety Code

  • UAE Fire and Life Safety Code of Practice, enforced by each emirate's Civil Defence
  • NFPA 13 widely used for sprinkler installation
  • Annual servicing by Civil Defence-approved contractors

Saudi Arabia: SBC 801

  • Section 903, Automatic Sprinkler Systems (pages 141 to 147 in the 2018 code)
  • Based on the 2015 International Fire Code with NFPA standards
  • Sprinklers relax other requirements, such as travel distance

India. NBC 2016 Part 4 sets sprinkler requirements in Table 7 by occupancy type and height (Table 7, Kerala Fire and Rescue Services copy). Delhi Fire Service states that sprinkler systems are designed and installed to IS 15105, with design plans and calculations submitted with the building plans (Delhi Fire Service FAQ).

NBCS 2026. BIS has published the National Building Construction Standards 2026 (SP 7:2026), reported as effective from 30 April 2026. Reports say fire and life safety norms now apply to residential buildings of 24 m and above (up from 15 m), and that "shall" has become "should" in the fire section (Drishti IAS; ULBA). Check which edition and state bye-laws apply to your project.

UAE. Sprinkler systems are governed by the UAE Fire and Life Safety Code of Practice and enforced by each emirate's Civil Defence (Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Sharjah). NFPA 13 is widely adopted, and annual servicing must be carried out by Civil Defence-approved contractors, with test certificates sent to the authority (BITO).

Saudi Arabia. SBC 801 covers automatic sprinkler systems in Section 903 (SBC 801 contents, 2018). The code is based on the 2015 International Fire Code and NFPA standards, and a sprinklered building gains relaxations elsewhere: longer travel distances, Class I instead of Class III standpipes, and hydrant distance extended from 120 m to 180 m (Pillai, ASRJETS 2025).

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the 4 types of fire sprinkler systems?
Wet pipe (water always in the pipes), dry pipe (pressurised air, for freezing areas), pre-action (water admitted only after detection, for sensitive spaces) and deluge (all heads open, for fast-spreading hazards).
What is the coverage area of one sprinkler?
For standard spray heads under NFPA 13, up to 225 ft² (20.9 m²) in light hazard with smooth noncombustible ceilings, 130 ft² (12.1 m²) in ordinary hazard, and 90 to 100 ft² (8.4 to 9.3 m²) in extra hazard and high-piled storage.
What is NFPA 13?
NFPA 13, Standard for the Installation of Sprinkler Systems, is the National Fire Protection Association standard for designing and installing sprinkler systems. The current edition is 2025, and it is widely used in the Gulf.
What is the difference between a wet and a dry sprinkler system?
A wet system keeps water in the pipes, so it discharges immediately but can freeze. A dry system holds pressurised air and lets water in only when a head opens, so it suits unheated spaces but responds more slowly and needs more maintenance.
How far apart should sprinklers be?
For standard spray heads, at most 15 ft (4.6 m) apart in light and ordinary hazard and 12 ft (3.7 m) in extra hazard, and at least 6 ft (1.8 m) apart. The distance to a wall must be no more than half the maximum spacing and no less than 4 in (102 mm).
What do sprinkler bulb colours mean?
They show the temperature at which the bulb bursts: orange 57 °C, red 68 °C, yellow 79 °C, green 93 °C, blue 141 °C, purple about 163 to 191 °C, and black for higher ratings.
