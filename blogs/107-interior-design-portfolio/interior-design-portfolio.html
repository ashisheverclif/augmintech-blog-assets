A strong interior design portfolio shows three to five of your best projects, strongest first, and for each one tells the story from brief to concept to drawings to finished result, with at least one set of proper working drawings and details. For a student or junior designer, 15 to 25 pages is usually enough, sent as a PDF small enough to email. Employers look for clear thinking, real technical drawings and software skill, not just pretty renders. Below is a page-by-page structure, what each project should include, examples of how to lay it out, and the mistakes that cost interviews.

TL;DR

  • Lead with your best project, not your first one. Reviewers often decide within the first few pages.
  • Show process, not only results: brief, site or user analysis, concept, zoning, plans, details, materials, renders.
  • Include real working drawings: furniture layout, reflected ceiling plan, elevations and at least one joinery detail.
  • Mix project types: one residential, one commercial or hospitality, and one detail-heavy or live project.
  • Keep the emailed PDF small (under about 10 MB is a safe target) and keep a fuller version online.
  • Tailor it for each job: a visualisation studio, a fit-out contractor and a design firm want different emphasis.

What Employers Actually Look For

A senior designer reviewing portfolios is usually busy and is asking five questions. Build every page to answer them:

Can you think?Do your concepts respond to the brief, the user and the space, or are they styles copied from Pinterest?
Can you draw?Are your plans, elevations and details correct, dimensioned and readable at scale?
Do you know materials?Can you specify finishes and show how they meet and are fixed?
Can you use our software?AutoCAD, SketchUp, a renderer, and increasingly Revit.
Will you need hand-holding?Clean, organised work signals that you can be trusted with a live project.
Did you do it yourself?Clearly state your role in group or office projects.

Portfolio pageWhat to include in your own work
01 · Project briefIdentify the building type, location, brief, project status and your role. Use one strong image to introduce the design.
02 · Site & contextShow site access, neighbouring buildings, orientation and constraints. Explain which observations influenced the design.

04 · Floor plansInclude readable plans with room names, circulation, entrances and key dimensions. Add scale and north direction where appropriate.
05 · Sections & elevationsChoose cuts that explain spatial relationships, levels, daylight and facade design. Coordinate them with the plans.

Technical Drawings: Where Most Portfolios Fall Short

Most student portfolios are heavy on renders and light on drawings. Yet the work a junior designer does every day is drawings: layouts, elevations, ceiling plans and details. A clean technical section is often what separates a shortlist from a rejection.

Furniture layout at 1:50 with key dimensions and clearances.
Reflected ceiling plan with ceiling heights, coves, bulkheads and light fittings with a legend.
Interior elevations of at least one room, showing finishes, joinery and electrical points.
One joinery detail at 1:10 or 1:5, such as a wardrobe or kitchen section, with materials labelled.
Flooring or finish plan with a legend or schedule.
Proper drafting: line weights, title block, scale bar, north point and consistent text.

Show drawings at a readable size; one drawing per half page is better than six tiny ones. If you have produced drawings in Revit, say so: a coordinated set with a room finish schedule or furniture schedule shows BIM skills that many larger firms value. Layout and Design Tips

  • Use a grid and keep margins, image positions and text blocks consistent on every page.
  • Pick one or two fonts and use size and weight, not many fonts, for hierarchy.
  • Let the work breathe: white space makes drawings and images easier to read.
  • Caption everything: drawing name, scale, software used and your role.
  • Keep colours neutral so your projects' own palettes stand out.
  • Number your pages so a reviewer can refer to "page 12" on the phone call.

10 Portfolio Mistakes That Cost Interviews

  1. Starting with your weakest or oldest work. Order by quality, not by semester.
  2. Only renders. No plans, elevations or details suggests you cannot produce working drawings.
  3. No explanation. Images without a brief or concept make reviewers guess what you were solving.
  4. Unclear authorship. Group or office work without your role stated raises doubts.
  5. Unreadable drawings. Tiny plans or thin grey lines that disappear on screen.
  6. A software list the pages do not prove. If you list Revit, show something made in Revit.
  7. A huge file. A 60 MB attachment may bounce or never be opened.
  8. Spelling and dimension errors. Small mistakes suggest careless drawings.
  9. Using other people's images without credit, or AI images presented as your own work.
  10. Sending the same portfolio everywhere. Reorder and trim for each employer.

Portfolio Tips for Gulf Jobs

Designers applying to Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Doha or Riyadh compete with an international pool, often for hotel, retail and office fit-out roles. Show fit-out detailing, ceiling and joinery drawings, and coordination with services, in metric units and clear English. Larger projects are increasingly BIM-led; Dubai has required BIM model submissions in IFC format for building permits on larger buildings since 1 January 2024, as summarised on Autodesk's Dubai BIM mandate page, so Revit drawings and schedules in your portfolio are a real advantage. Keep the PDF short and attach a one-page CV.

Turn One Project Into Evidence

Consider a self-initiated reading lounge for two adults. Start with the user need: comfortable reading, storage and a clear entry route. Show two early layouts, identify the rejected option and explain the trade-off. Follow with the selected plan, one internal elevation, a joinery detail and the final visual.

Write a specific contribution statement: “Concept project; I developed the layout, model, finish schedule and presentation. Furniture assets are credited separately.” Replace this wording with your actual role. Do not describe a model as built work or show another person’s drawing as your own.

Software Evidence That an Interviewer Can Inspect

Include a cropped view of your own model or drawing only when it proves something useful. For AutoCAD, that might be organised layers and a correctly plotted detail. For Revit, show a room schedule beside the relevant plan and explain the parameters used. For a renderer, show one lighting study and the reason for the selected exposure. Label the application and your contribution; a software logo by itself proves no skill.

Keep client names, addresses and confidential drawings out of public files unless you have permission. Test the final PDF on a phone and laptop, reopen its sharing link while signed out, and keep editable source files ready for a private review. If a drawing is unreadable at normal viewing size, give it its own page instead of shrinking it further.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many projects should an interior design portfolio have?
Three to five well-presented projects is a good range for students and junior designers. Lead with your strongest and make sure they show different project types.
How many pages should an interior design portfolio be?
For students and juniors, 15 to 25 pages usually works for the emailed version, with a fuller version online. Experienced designers may go longer but should still keep it focused.
Should I include hand sketches?
Yes, briefly. A page of concept sketches shows how you think. But they should support, not replace, your drawings and final images.
Can I include college projects in a job portfolio?
Yes, especially as a fresher. Present them like real projects, with a brief, drawings and outcome. As you gain experience, replace them with built work.
What software should I use to make my portfolio?
InDesign is the common professional choice for layouts; Illustrator, Affinity Publisher or even well-set templates also work. What matters is a consistent grid and readable drawings.
Is it OK to use AI-generated images?
Only as clearly labelled concept or mood images, if at all. Never present AI images as your own renders or design work; reviewers notice, and it damages trust.
Should my portfolio be online or a PDF?
Both. Send a compact PDF with your application and keep a fuller version online, on Behance, Issuu or a personal site, with a link on your CV and in the PDF.
